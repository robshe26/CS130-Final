# The Cost of Extra Traction: Front-Wheel Drive vs. The World 

Living in Buffalo comes with many advantages: the sports, the friendliness, and the community. However, living in this area also comes with its disadvantages, namely the weather. Buffalo is home to some of the worst winter weather, being ranked the 4th snowiest city in America by Fox Weather. When it comes to driving in the winter, this makes it pretty difficult facing ice, snow, and blizzards. To combat this, people buy Four-Wheel Drive and All-Wheel Drive vehicles for extra security. Even dealerships heavily market these systems as absolute necessities for winter survival. Consumers gladly check the box for the AWD upgrade, viewing it as a vital safety feature for those snowy days. 

However, these additional spinning wheels require a sacrifice. Sending power to all four wheels, rather than just two, requires heavy drivetrains and an engine that is forced to work harder. By analyzing the data of over 5,000 vehicles, by Austin Cody Bart, we began to uncover what this extra traction is costing us at the pump every day. 

### The Traction Tax

When we look at the combined average of city miles per gallon and highway miles per gallon across the different drivelines, there is a clear difference. Front-Wheel Drive is clearly the most efficient option, with around 5 more miles per gallon than its next counterpart. The moment that you upgrade to All-Wheel drive, the traction tax comes into effect, losing these 5 miles per gallon instantly. For a driver that commutes to work or school, this difference is noticeable, and can cost hundreds of extra dollars in fuel annually. If you step up to a heavier Four-Wheel Drive systems, commonly found in trucks and big SUVs, the averages plummet even further with an almost 10 mile per gallon difference. 

### The Highway Bonus

There is a general expectation that once we are no longer in traffic, rather than cruising on the highway, we get additional miles per gallon. That is why cars are often advertised with a city and highway miles per gallon. However, the extra mechanical components of All-Wheel drive and Four-Wheel drive systems ruin this highway potential. 



The chart above shows this "MPG Gap" which is the extra miles per gallon that a vehicle gains when transitioning from the city to the highway. The chart visualizes the calculated hidden friction of driving with all four wheels compared to just the front two. A lightweight Front-Wheel drive setup allows the vehicle additional mileage on the highway, granting this "highway bonus" of over 8 extra MPG compared to city driving. However, the heavier drivetrains of All-Wheel drive and Four-Wheel drive limit this  cruising efficiency. All-Wheel drive vehicles only gain around 6.8 extra MPG and Four-Wheel drive systems limit that bonus to just 5 MPG extra. 

### The Downside to Front-Wheel Drive

The only downside to the Front-Wheel drive systems come with its power. This is what makes the decision for Buffalo residents so difficult. There is clearly money to be saved when choosing a Front-Wheel drive car, but is the loss of power worth it? 

The scatter plot maps out Horsepower against the average MPG, color coded by driveline. The blue dots represent Front-Wheel drive which are overwhelmingly on the left side with less horsepower. This is because these cars require much less power to get two wheels moving versus their counterparts. When looking at the spread of Four-Wheel drive vehicles and All-wheel drive vehicles, they fall more towards the middle and right side of the chart. This means that they require more power to be able to operate. 


### Is the MPG penalty worth it?

This additional power, and the traction that comes with four wheels moving makes them advantageous in the winter time. This begs the question: _Is the tradeoff in gas mileage worth the extra safety in the winter?_

For most people, I feel the answer to this would be no. While it is helpful, in my experience driving a Front-Wheel drive car in the winter has not been an issue. For unexperienced drivers in winter weather it might be advantageous, but for people that live in Buffalo and regularly drive in the snow, a Front-Wheel drive is enough. More tires moving on the road does not give you extra stopping power, in fact it might make it worse because of the added weight. The roads are mostly plowed, and serious weather events only happen a select few number of times a year. So, in my case the tradeoff between the extra money in gas, versus the extra traction is not worth it

__For others it may come down to your specific reality:__
- __The Need:__ If you are a first responder, a plow driver, or live on top of a big hill, the ability to gain extra traction and acceleration is a must have. For these indiviuals the "traction tax" may be worth it for the added piece of mind.
- __The Average Commuter:__ For the average commuter who is driving on treated roads and cant afford to wait untill the plows come or a whiteout passes, the worth factor changes. Paying for a lower fuel economy 365 days a year to solve a traction problem that only exists a few days a year is a poor return on investment.

### The Verdict

For most people, the shift from Front-Wheel drive to any other driveline is not needed. While these other drivelines provide assistance during a massive snowstorm, consumers must know that they are paying a daily penalty for it. Buying an All-Wheel Drive or Four-Wheel drive vehicle means accepting a less efficient, reduced highway gas mileage, and a higher fuel cost for the other 350 days of the year. Another option for added safety in the winter would be to install winter tires. In this case, this is the best of both worlds. You get the efficiency of a lightweight, two-wheel-drive system all summer, and the grip needed for safety in the winter.
